From idea to validated solution

How do you turn assumptions into requirements?

A good requirement connects a parameter with function, conditions, a verification method and a decision.

“The joint needs to be strong.” It sounds reasonable. But what should a design engineer do with that sentence? Calculate capacity for 10, 40 or 100 kN? In tension, bending, temperature or impact? And how will we know whether the result is sufficient?


Requirements often arise by adding a number to a broad wish. “It needs to be light” becomes “mass below 2 kg”. That is progress, but a number alone still does not guarantee shared understanding.


A good requirement connects a parameter with function, conditions, a verification method and a decision. It also has a source: we know where it came from and which assumptions support it. Without that, we are still firing requirements at the engineer blindly and hoping they will somehow create an exceptional product.

1. A requirement does not come from a round number

In my engineering thesis, the key value was 40 kN. That was the axial load the joint between a carbon-fibre tube and an aluminium end fitting was expected to carry.


It was not merely a number. Behind it sat a line of reasoning: defining the directions of forces on the control arm, describing load scenarios and checking how automotive experts approached the subject. Only then did a value emerge for calculations and testing.

The order matters: function and use case first, then data sources and assumptions, and finally the number.


Requirements come from several directions. A user describes a need; architecture imposes interfaces; physics determines loads; manufacturing defines realistic tolerances. Budget, regulation and service add further constraints. The team must convert these messages into a shared form and retain their origin.

When someone asks three months later, “why 40 kN?”, “because it was in Excel” cannot be the answer. The source lets us assess whether the number still applies after a change to vehicle mass, geometry or use. Looking back, I do not remember exactly where my 40 kN came from — it was 2018 and I was a student.


In a mature project, we should be able to trace a requirement back to the need and data that justify it, and trace a changed assumption forward to the requirements, calculations and tests it affects. In a small team, a table with an identifier, rationale and verification method is enough. The tool is secondary; the ability to reconstruct decision logic is what matters.

2. Requirement, goal, constraint and assumption are four different things

In project conversations, everything likes to land in one bag labelled “assumptions”. That is convenient until a decision is needed.


A requirement defines a condition the solution must meet. A goal says what we want to improve. A constraint defines the space in which we can move. An assumption is a temporary simplification used to start an analysis. For the control arm, this could look like:

  • goal: reduce unsprung mass;
  • requirement: the joint must carry a defined axial load;
  • constraint: the solution must fit the suspension geometry and existing mounting points;
  • assumption: under correct system operation, the control arm is loaded mainly axially in tension and compression.

These statements have different roles. A goal cannot always be passed or failed. A requirement leads to a compliant or non-compliant result. A constraint can eliminate entire families of solutions, and a false assumption can invalidate calculations.


The most dangerous assumptions are those disguised as facts. “The control arm only works axially” simplifies a model, but it depends on correct geometry and suspension operation. Clearance, manufacturing variation or impact can add bending. That does not mean every model must cover every phenomenon in the world. It means the simplification needs a label, so we know when to trust it and what to check separately.

3. A good requirement can be disproved

This sounds counterintuitive, but a requirement becomes useful only when we can clearly show that a solution does not meet it. “The joint should be sufficiently strong” can always be defended. “The joint specimen shall carry 40 kN in an axial tensile test without loss of capacity before that value” can fail a test. That is precisely why it supports decisions.

A complete requirement should state at least:

  • what it applies to;
  • the state and conditions in which it applies;
  • the parameter being assessed;
  • the acceptance limit;
  • how and at which stage it will be confirmed.

Add a sixth question: what will we do with a borderline result? Otherwise, after testing, the acceptance criterion starts being creatively negotiated in the direction favoured by the current prototype.


A requirement need not contain a full laboratory procedure, but it must be verifiable. A tolerance must be measurable and a load must be linked to a case. Different requirements need different methods: measurement, calculation, simulation, demonstration or test. Choose the method together with the requirement.

This quickly exposes statements we cannot check. “Easy assembly” can be described by time, number of operations, required tools or opportunity for error. Not every characteristic needs one magic number, but the team should know how it will settle a dispute.

4. A part meets requirements only together with the process that creates it

With a bonded joint, it is easy to focus on bond length, diameter and adhesive strength from a datasheet. Those parameters look good in an equation. Actual capacity also depends on surface preparation, clearance, centring, adhesive amount and distribution, curing conditions, material storage and repeatability of the work.


For my thesis, I built three sleeve variants. I cured them in my mum’s oven — she still remembers the smell and the cleaning procedure. I made the sleeves as well as I could at the time, which was average. After curing, I rejected two specimens of the simplest variant because the adhesive pushed uncentred sleeves out of the tubes. Bonding and surface-preparation issues also appeared; mechanical sanding damaged the composite structure.


The lesson was valuable: a product requirement without a process requirement can be only a wish. We can require 40 kN, but without control of critical characteristics every unit becomes a different experiment. Surface preparation, adhesive batch and sleeve alignment will affect the result.

Functional requirements therefore need process requirements alongside them. Identify the parameters that genuinely control the outcome: surface preparation, allowable clearance, datum method, curing time and temperature, material-batch identification and inspection criteria. This matters especially in bonding, welding, laminating, heat treatment and 3D printing. In such processes, final geometry does not tell the full story of a part.

5. Requirements form a network of trade-offs

One requirement can be optimised fairly easily. The trouble begins when a product must be light, strong, small, inexpensive and simple to make at the same time. Which is almost always.


An initial bonded-joint calculation, using manufacturer data and assumed factors, indicated a bond length above 100 mm. That was hard to reconcile with low mass and a compact design. Based on recommendations for improving geometry and reducing assembly errors, a shorter variant was selected for testing.

This moved part of the confidence from a catalogue calculation to geometry, process quality and test results. That is how design trade-offs work. Tightening one requirement often raises cost, mass or manufacturing difficulty. Relaxing a geometric constraint may improve strength. Choosing a simpler sleeve can shorten machining but worsen centring and adhesive distribution.


Requirements need priorities. We need to know which are absolute, which we optimise and which can be negotiated. Otherwise, all of them become “important”, and the last loud topic in a meeting wins. Separate mandatory conditions from the optimisation space first. Capacity, interface compatibility and regulations are gates. Only then should compliant variants be compared for mass, cost, manufacturing time and assembly ease.

Rather than creating the “best solution” in a vacuum, we first seek an acceptable solution and then the best one according to explicit criteria. Requirements form a system of dependencies, not a list of independent wishes.

A requirement is a decision tool, not documentation decoration

A good requirement lets two people reach the same assessment of a solution. It does not need to remove all uncertainty, but it should make clear where that uncertainty sits.

Before detailed design starts, check whether:

  • every critical number has a source and rationale;
  • goals, requirements, constraints and assumptions are not mixed together;
  • operating conditions and verification method are clear;
  • requirements cover critical process characteristics, not just part geometry;
  • priorities and trade-offs are recorded before a result appears.

Requirements are not tablets carved once and forever. When a test or analysis reveals a new phenomenon, update them together with their source, revision and impact on the project. That is not disorder. It is controlled learning.

  • Are the operating conditions of every requirement unambiguous?
  • Are the parameter and acceptance limit measurable?
  • Are the verification method and stage agreed before solution assessment?

  • Do requirements cover process characteristics critical to the result, not only geometry?
