Beyond the CAD screen

The most expensive mistakes happen before the first prototype

How to clarify requirements, tests, materials and manufacturing realities before a project enters costly prototype iterations.

Before the first prototype is built

We often treat the first prototype as the moment when a project truly meets reality for the first time. This is when we see whether the mechanism works, the parts fit together and the device meets its basic assumptions. The problem is that many of the most expensive mistakes arise much earlier—not during machining, assembly or testing, but when the team makes its first decisions: what exactly is to be built, under what conditions it must operate, how the outcome will be assessed and how the product will be manufactured.

If those decisions remain unclear, the CAD model may be correct and the prototype may look convincing, yet the project can still begin to drift. Subsequent iterations are no longer solving one technical problem; they are trying to compensate for the lack of a shared product definition. Before costly design work begins, four areas are therefore worth closing: measurable requirements, acceptance criteria, the relationship between material and process, and the involvement of manufacturing and suppliers. The aim is not to create a perfect specification. It is to ensure that the team knows which decisions are deliberate and which remain hypotheses.

1. A requirement that cannot be measured is difficult to verify

“It must be strong”, “easy to assemble”, “as light as possible” or “inexpensive to manufacture” may sound like requirements. In practice, they are directions, and every member of the team may understand them differently. To a designer, “light” may mean a 15% mass reduction. To a product manager, it may mean no transport difficulties. To the user, it may mean being able to carry the device with one hand. Each interpretation leads to different material, design and cost decisions.

Imprecise requirements have another characteristic: conflicts between them emerge late. The product is expected to be stiff, light, inexpensive, weather-resistant and available quickly at the same time. Modelling alone cannot resolve that combination. Priorities must be established and trade-offs accepted. A good technical brief should therefore not be a wish list. Among other things, it should answer:

  • what function the product must perform and under what conditions;
  • which parameters are critical and which are negotiable;
  • the permissible manufacturing cost and the production volume behind it;
  • the constraints imposed by size, mass, transport, power supply or the operating environment;
  • what “good enough” means for the first version of the product.

The budget should be treated as a technical requirement, not as something to review at the end. If the design is directed from the outset towards processes, materials or tolerances that are unsuitable for the planned volume, later “cost optimisation” may in fact mean redesigning the product.

3 review questions

  • Would two people outside the design team test the requirement in the same way and reach the same result?
  • Is it clear which requirements are critical, which are negotiable and which trade-offs the team is prepared to accept?
  • Have cost, volume, mass, operating conditions and other constraints been defined precisely enough to influence design decisions?

2. Acceptance criteria must be defined before the test, not after it

A prototype cannot answer a question that was never asked. It may work during a presentation, pass several trials and still fail to provide the information the team needs to make a decision. “It works” is a particularly dangerous result because it sounds final. In reality, it may mean only that one unit worked once, under specific conditions and with the involvement of people who knew its limitations.

Acceptance criteria bring order to this uncertainty. They define what constitutes a positive result, the conditions under which the test is conducted, what is measured and what decision follows a positive or negative outcome. In practice, at least three questions should be separated:

  • Does the solution perform its function?
  • Does it perform that function over the required range and under the required conditions?
  • Is the result repeatable enough to proceed to the next stage?

This distinction changes how a prototype is designed. If the first iteration is intended to verify load capacity and stiffness, there is no need to invest immediately in a production-like finish. If the objective is to assess assembly, the prototype must reproduce interfaces, tool access and the sequence of operations. If the team is comparing variants, it needs a common method and identical test conditions.

In projects where I prepared test protocols, reports, acceptance criteria and test fixtures, the document itself was not the greatest source of value. What mattered was agreeing before the trial what we actually wanted to learn. This turned the test into a decision-making tool rather than merely an event in the schedule.

3 review questions

  • Before building the prototype, can we define a result that will allow us to say “proceed”, “iterate” or “reject this concept”?
  • Is it clear exactly what will be measured, under what conditions and according to which method?
  • Does the prototype test a specific hypothesis and provide the information required for the next design decision?

3. A material cannot be selected in isolation from the process

Material selection is often reduced to comparing mechanical properties. In reality, the material is part of a larger system of decisions. What matters is not only what it can withstand, but also how it will be formed, joined, finished, inspected and sourced. The same function may be provided by a machined, sheet-metal, cast, welded or polymer component. Each process creates different geometry, constraints, tolerances, tooling costs and supply risks.

For an initial prototype, a machined part may be the fastest way to verify function. This does not mean that geometry optimised for machining will suit casting or injection moulding. If the target process is known, its logic should be incorporated as early as possible. Otherwise, a successful prototype may merely postpone an expensive redesign. While working on systems combining injection-moulded plastics, sheet metal, castings and machined parts, I repeatedly saw how a material decision affected the entire product. It changed support conditions, joints, cable routing, assembly order, component availability and the test plan. Material assessment should therefore consider:

  • function and loads;
  • the target process and expected production volume;
  • tolerances that the process can maintain;
  • availability of stock material and suppliers;
  • how the part will be joined to other components;
  • finish, operating environment and servicing;
  • the cost of the complete solution, not only the price per kilogram of material.

3 review questions

  • Have we selected a material, or a complete method of manufacturing, joining and inspecting the part?
  • Are the geometry, tolerances, joining method and inspection approach realistic for the selected process?
  • Does the decision account for material and supplier availability and the full cost of the solution—including tooling, processing, finishing, inspection and potential scrap?

4. Manufacturing and suppliers should not see the design for the first time at the end

The design team does not need to know every detail of every process. It does, however, need to know when to invite people who do. Consulting manufacturing or a supplier too late often leads to more than a minor correction. It may reveal unavailable tooling, an unsuitable bend radius, a fixture problem, a difficult-to-inspect dimension, an impractical tolerance or a component with an unacceptable lead time.

Early involvement of a manufacturing partner does not transfer responsibility for the design. It is a way to identify constraints sooner. A short conversation during the concept stage can be more valuable than an extensive review once the documentation is complete and the prototype deadline has been agreed. Regulatory and safety requirements should be treated in the same way. Not every product is regulated, but if it operates in a specific environment or interacts with users, electrical energy, loads or foreseeable misuse, those requirements should influence the architecture from the outset. Reviewing them only before launch can undermine earlier design decisions.

A useful minimum is a concept review involving representatives of design, testing, manufacturing, purchasing and—where required—quality or compliance. The purpose is not to hold a large status meeting. It is to identify assumptions that one function regards as obvious and another considers unrealistic.

3 review questions

  • Who outside the project team could disprove our most important assumption today at the lowest cost?
  • Have manufacturing, testing, purchasing, suppliers and—where necessary—quality and compliance had an opportunity to assess the concept before the documentation is frozen?
  • Are manufacturing constraints, lead times, safety and compliance requirements shaping the product architecture now, or have they been left until the end?

A prototype should test hypotheses, not organise chaos

It is impossible to eliminate all uncertainty before the first prototype—and that is not the objective. Product development always requires iteration. The difference lies in whether we iterate deliberately around a specific hypothesis or use successive prototypes to discover fundamental requirements.

Before modelling begins and parts are ordered, five points are worth confirming:

  • critical requirements are measurable and have owners;
  • priorities and trade-offs are explicit;
  • the team knows what will be tested and how;
  • the material has been considered together with the process, volume and supply chain;
  • manufacturing, suppliers and safety requirements have been involved at the right stage.

If these elements are in order, the first prototype does not have to be perfect. It must provide information that allows the team to make the next good decision.
