Beyond the CAD screen

The prototype works. What must happen before production begins?

How to move from a working prototype to a repeatable product: testing, documentation, change control, cost, assembly and lifecycle.

A working prototype is only the beginning

The prototype works. The mechanism moves, the structure carries the load and the device does what it was meant to do. At last, there is something more to show than a render and a slide with the schedule. A great moment. There really is something to celebrate. Just do not open the bottle labelled “mass production” yet.


A working prototype confirms that the main concept makes sense. It does not automatically confirm that the product can be built again, to the same quality, at the target cost and without a designer standing beside the workstation. A unit assembled by R&D often relies on knowledge that appears on no drawing. This needs a little filing, that cable has to be routed “in this particular way”, and that bolt must be tightened by feel. The team knows every previous iteration, so it may not even notice how much information exists only in people's heads. Manufacturing should not need that context.

Moving from “it works” to “we can manufacture it repeatedly” requires five areas to be closed: validation, inspection criteria, documentation and change control, the cost of the whole process and the product's life after it leaves the factory.

1. Do not keep testing what you already know

The first prototype usually answers the basic question: does this concept work at all? The next tests should target risks. Otherwise, you can confirm the same thing ten times and never test the condition that eventually brings the product down.

A validation plan is best started with a list of key requirements and risks. For each one, establish:

  • exactly what is to be confirmed;
  • which unit, configuration and conditions are representative;
  • what will be measured and with what accuracy;
  • how many repetitions are needed to support the decision;
  • what result means success;
  • what happens if the result is borderline or negative.

Not every test has to look like a space programme. Sometimes a simple fixture that applies a load repeatedly and captures the result is enough. In other cases, several factors need to be compared, for example through design of experiments, or DOE. The scale of the test should follow from the decision we need to make, not from a desire to fill the report with charts.


On electromechanical products, I prepared test protocols and reports, acceptance criteria and fixtures supporting production tests. The common thread was always the same: a requirement has to become a repeatable method. Without that, even a hundred trials can produce a lot of numbers and very few answers.

2. Inspection must work without a designer looking over your shoulder

During prototyping, many things are done “by feel”. The designer knows how to route a cable, how tightly to secure a joint and which sound is normal. That is manageable for one unit. In production, it becomes a lottery. Knowledge stored in someone's head must become a way of working that can be communicated, repeated and inspected.


“Matches the model” is not much of a criterion here. Critical characteristics, a measurement method and decision limits need to be defined. The team must also check whether the selected dimension can actually be measured reliably in production. A requirement that cannot be inspected is a little like a rulebook written in invisible ink. A good control plan covers three levels:

  • Part: were the critical characteristics manufactured in accordance with the documentation?
  • Assembly: does the way it is joined and adjusted produce the correct result?
  • Product: does the completed unit perform its function and meet safety requirements?

If the product is checked only at the very end, a defect may be found after the full cost of parts and assembly has already been incurred. If we measure everything that can possibly be measured, inspection becomes expensive and slow. The aim is to inspect the right characteristics at a point in the process where we can still react sensibly.


The simplest rule? Someone who did not take part in developing the product should be able to use the documentation and say: good, bad or needs a decision. Without calling the designer to ask, “What exactly did the author mean?”.

3. A folder called “FINAL_final_really-final2” is not configuration control

A prototype may be built from a mixture of models, drawings, notes, messages and manual corrections. In a small team, everyone more or less knows what changed. “More or less” stops working quickly as the number of parts, suppliers, workstations and units grows.

Production readiness requires one coherent information package. Depending on the product, it may include:

  • approved models and drawings with revisions;
  • the EBOM, or engineering bill of materials;
  • specifications for standard components;
  • assembly instructions and required tightening torques;
  • inspection and test procedures;
  • marking, packaging and transport requirements;
  • a record of deviations and changes.

Having the files does not mean that the configuration is under control. We need to know which revision applies, who approved it, from which unit it should be used and what else changes with it. In a PLM environment, ECR and ECO processes, engineering change requests and engineering change orders, are used for this purpose. Their point is not to produce forms. It is to carry a change through the whole product, because one decision can affect the model, drawing, BOM, inventory, assembly instructions and test at the same time.


Working with Arena PLM, EBOM structures and change processes taught me that the tool itself does not solve the problem. Responsibility comes first: who submits the change, who assesses its impact, who approves it and how manufacturing learns when to start building the new version. PLM helps keep things in order. It cannot replace a decision that nobody made.

4. A cheap part can make an expensive product

Once the prototype works, cost reduction quickly enters the conversation. The easiest thing is to compare two part prices and choose the lower one. But the component price is only one piece of the bill. A cheaper item may need an extra bracket, longer assembly, separate inspection or more frequent rework. A more expensive one may remove two bolts, simplify locating and shorten the test. The question is not “which part is cheaper?”, but “which solution gives us the lower total product cost?”.

It is worth calculating, among other things:

  • the number of operations and tool changes during assembly;
  • tasks that are prone to mistakes;
  • the cost of special fixtures;
  • inspection and test time;
  • the number of variants held in inventory;
  • the supplier's ability to maintain the required tolerances;
  • how cost behaves at the target volume.

This is where DFMA, design for manufacture and assembly, becomes a business tool. Removing a part, simplifying locating, improving access or reducing the possibility of incorrect assembly can shorten time, improve quality and reduce risk at the same time.


When launching products into volume production and building procedures for R&D and manufacturing, the most important step was moving from one working unit to a process that produces the same result repeatedly. A one-off “cost-down” round will not achieve that. It takes cooperation between design, purchasing, quality, suppliers and assembly. A part price is easy to enter in a spreadsheet. The cost of chaos is a little harder, but that does not mean it is not there.

5. A product does not stop existing when it leaves the line

A finished product still has to be packaged, transported, installed, used, serviced and eventually withdrawn. Every one of those stages can expose an assumption that the laboratory prototype never tested. Packaging is a great example. A product may work perfectly and then fail to survive transport, or require a very expensive crate because nobody planned the support points, lifting method or vulnerable components.


On a project for a client in the United States, packaging was part of the scope alongside design, cost and manufacturing coordination. It was not an extra added at the end. It was a condition for delivering the solution in one piece, which, let us agree, is quite an important product function.

The same applies to service. Access to wear parts, diagnostic capability, disassembly time and revision identification have a direct effect on after-sales cost. If we consider them only after design freeze, a small service improvement may require a new enclosure, wiring changes or a different subsystem architecture.


Before production begins, it is therefore worth walking the product through its complete scenario:

  • receipt of components;
  • storage and identification;
  • assembly and testing;
  • packaging and transport;
  • installation at the customer's site;
  • use, service and change management;
  • withdrawal or replacement.

If any stage requires improvisation, we have just found another design risk.

When does a prototype really become a product?

There is no ceremonial button that turns a prototype into a product. Readiness appears when testing, documentation and process begin to form one coherent system.


Before scaling, confirm that:

  • key requirements have test results and clear criteria;
  • critical characteristics can be manufactured and inspected repeatedly;
  • the documentation describes one approved configuration;
  • changes have owners and a controlled implementation method;
  • cost has been calculated for the product, assembly, quality and service;
  • packaging, transport, installation and the lifecycle do not depend on improvisation.

A working prototype is important evidence. A production-ready product requires something more: a way to achieve the same result repeatedly, through different people and different suppliers, including when the R&D team is not standing nearby.
